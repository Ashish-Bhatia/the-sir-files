What is SIR?

A voter list records who is registered to vote in a constituency. Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, is a process for preparing that list afresh. It checks the entries that should remain, adds eligible people who are missing and removes entries that no longer qualify.

The Election Commission directs the exercise. Booth Level Officers carry out fieldwork. Electoral Registration Officers decide registration questions. A name missing from a draft list is not automatically a final exclusion: claims, notices, hearings and publication come at different stages.

The basic sequence is field verification, a draft list, claims and objections, decisions and a final list. Later applications and appeals are separate steps. A voter identity card, a submitted application and an entry on the current roll are different things.

ECI order and procedure

Before 2025: the law and the older lists

The Representation of the People Act, 1950 sets the legal framework for electoral rolls. The Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 provide the procedures. SIR operates within that framework.

Older intensive-revision lists are used as historical references. Their dates differ by state, including 2002–06 references in the present exercise. A link to an old list helps trace a record. Its absence alone does not prove that someone is not a citizen or is ineligible today.

The date of a legal document matters too. The ECI compilation located here is dated July 2025 and includes the 2021 amendments. Later changes and current rule versions still require checking.

June–September 2025: SIR begins in Bihar

On 24 June 2025, the ECI ordered SIR, beginning with Bihar. The process brought questions about acceptable documents, citizenship checks, access to hearings and the time available to respond.

Bihar’s draft roll appeared on 1 August. About 65 lakh entries from the earlier roll were absent from that draft. The final roll was published on 30 September with around 7.42 crore voters.

Those two figures describe different stages. Additions and further removals took place between draft and final publication. The draft omissions therefore cannot be treated as a one-to-one count of people finally or wrongly excluded.

The legal challenge continued. The Supreme Court’s final judgment on the Bihar framework came in May 2026, discussed below.

October 2025–February 2026: the exercise expands

A further multi-jurisdiction phase followed on 27 October 2025. Each jurisdiction had its own timetable. A date or figure from one state should not be assumed to apply elsewhere.

In January and February 2026, the Supreme Court issued West Bengal-specific directions on discrepancy notices and unresolved cases. Those orders describe the procedures the Court required. They do not, by themselves, establish whether every later decision followed those procedures.

Goa published its final roll on 21 February. Its signed report permits a precise draft-to-final check:

1,084,992 + 12,166 − 3,812 − 35,780 = 1,057,566

The calculation brings together the draft, accepted additions and two removal categories. It balances. That establishes the accounting, rather than the correctness of every individual eligibility decision.

May 2026: a wider phase and the Supreme Court judgment

On 14 May, the ECI issued directions covering 19 states and Union Territories. The order set state-specific reference years and schedules. It also stated that documents were not to be collected during enumeration. Later directions and revised schedules must be read separately from these original targets.

On 27 May, the Supreme Court upheld the Bihar SIR framework. Its judgment also set legal bounds on citizenship-related inquiries and the handling of such cases.

The distinction is important. Upholding the framework does not decide every local notice, every later form or every exclusion. Those actions remain questions of their own facts and applicable law.

July–September 2026: local schedules and safeguards change

The original national schedules were not the last word. Uttarakhand’s July operational bulletin clarified a later notice window. Tripura’s later timetable was reported by the public broadcaster, but the signed replacement order has not been obtained here.

Tripura also exposed a mistake in an earlier version of this site. The original signed annexure gave a claims window from 21 October to 20 November. Our earlier allegation of an impossible deadline was wrong and was corrected.

On 26 September, the Commission announced doorstep document collection directions, follow-up steps for left-out voters and an independent-expert review of ECINet. ECINet is the digital system involved in the process. An announced review is not a published audit report.

1–7 October 2026: a drive to reach people left out

On 1 October, the ECI instructed officials to compare older and current rolls, visit absent electors and help eligible applicants. This was a direction to carry out follow-up work. Its announcement is not a count of completed re-registrations.

Uttarakhand’s final-publication report, dated 3 October, allows all 13 district equations to be checked. The totals reconcile: 7,133,785 + 129,872 − 104,708 = 7,158,949. These are admitted claims and objections, not a measured count of wrongful exclusions and restorations.

Goa announced special enrolment camps on 7 October. The result of a camp still needs to be traced from application to decision and then to the published roll.

9 October 2026: limits on Form 7 objections

Form 7 is used for objections to inclusion and requests for deletion. The ECI’s October order limits an individual to ten applications during a claims period and five per month during continuous updating. Separate rules apply to party-appointed Booth Level Agents.

The existence of a bulk objection does not establish that a name was deleted. The next records to examine are the notice, the voter’s response, the ERO’s decision and the resulting roll.

This distinction is central to the Karnataka material. The local criminal-proceeding developments remain reported evidence until the authenticated orders and original objection records are checked.

12–14 October 2026: deadlines and the Form 6 dispute

Maharashtra’s signed extension set 26 October as the revised claims-and-objections deadline. The actual Gazette issue remains unverified here. Its application bulletin reports intake, not a total of people restored to the roll.

Meanwhile, Form 6 raised a different problem. The standard form contains a first-time inclusion declaration. Previously registered voters seeking re-inclusion questioned how that wording applied to them. The exact challenged online version and the broader signed Supreme Court record remain separate missing documents.

In its 14 October order concerning Nilotpal Datta and another petitioner, the Delhi High Court allowed the two applicants to submit Form 6 for processing under law. It rejected the first petitioner’s concern about a false-declaration accusation in the circumstances recorded. The order does not record completed inclusion or settle every issue about the form.

15–17 October 2026: another revision stage and Delhi follow-up

On 15 October, the ECI scheduled a Special Summary Revision, or SSR, in 17 jurisdictions. The order deferred schedules for 16 others and stated that SIR had not started in three. SSR is a separate stage, rather than a new name for every SIR outcome.

The order’s scheduled dates are a draft on 12 November, claims through 11 December, disposal by 28 December and final publication on 4 January 2027, with 1 January 2027 as the qualifying date. These are the targets in that order, not a live deadline service.

Further litigation about the Commission’s decision-making was reported in October. A reported notice calling for a response is not a finding that the allegations are proved.

On 17 October, reporting described Delhi’s re-verification drive covering about 47.5 lakh draft omissions. The signed local instruction and matched inclusion results have not been inspected here.

Where things stand

The published material establishes orders, timetables, court decisions and some reliable checks of official totals. It does not establish how many eligible people nationwide were wrongly excluded.

To answer an individual question, follow the whole sequence: was the person eligible, what notice was served, what response was received, what decision was made, and did the correct entry appear on the roll? An application count or a software flag does not answer all of those questions.
